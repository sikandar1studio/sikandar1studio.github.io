Planning an interactive event game: beyond the main screen

The gameplay screen is only one part of an event experience. A visitor also needs to notice the activity, understand how to join, learn the controls and recognize when the round has ended. Planning those moments together gives the project a clearer brief.

By Sikandar Riaz4 November 20264 min read
IN THIS GUIDE

THE STARTING POINT

Brief the whole round, including joining and resetting. The experience has to make sense to the next person in the queue as well as the person already playing.

1. Decide what the interaction is for

Start with the event objective and the audience. Is the interaction an invitation to explore a subject, a short competitive challenge, or a way to draw attention to a demonstration? Choose the main purpose before adding mechanics, information or registration steps.

Describe the expected circumstances: whether people approach alone or in groups, how much explanation they can reasonably receive, and whether an operator is present. These decisions influence the joining flow and the amount of information each screen needs to carry. A brief for an exhibition in Riyadh should include the venue conditions rather than assuming a standard installation setup.

2. Draw the complete visitor journey

Map the invitation, joining, instructions, countdown, play, result and return to the starting state. Not every experience needs all of these as separate screens, but each moment needs an intentional answer. Think about what is visible while nobody is playing and what changes when a participant arrives.

The Memory / Match case study illustrates this approach through entry, registration, countdown and end-of-round artboards. Those screens make the beginning and ending visible as design work. They should be read as interface evidence, rather than as proof of a particular event's attendance or operational performance.

3. Give each screen a clear responsibility

A shared display and a personal phone are useful for different parts of an experience. The shared screen can hold the scene, player positions and overall progress. The phone can keep the participant's identity and immediate controls close at hand. Decide which information belongs where before designing both layouts.

Island Rush provides a visual example: its selected captures show four game environments, five phone-controller previews and a character-running loop. The controller layouts group steering and larger action buttons around the player's identity. The useful lesson is the separation of responsibilities between screens; a new implementation still needs its own device and network checks.

4. Plan the moments when something changes

Ask what happens when a player joins late, puts down a phone, loses a connection or leaves before a round ends. Decide how an operator can restart the experience and how the next group knows it is ready. These are scope questions, not details to leave until the event opens.

For a timed activity, define the meaning of completion and timeout. Make feedback readable and give the participant an obvious next step. Any information collected during joining should have a clear purpose, owner and handling plan; a game does not automatically need a registration form.

5. Make the venue part of the production brief

Collect the display resolution and orientation, viewing distance, playback or application machine, control devices and network arrangement. Include setup access, operator responsibilities and the opportunity for a rehearsal. If several screens or devices must work together, list their relationships explicitly.

Testing should follow the actual journey from a fresh start through a complete round and reset. Rehearse the failure and recovery cases that matter to the venue, as well as normal play. A portfolio screenshot, rendered stand or animation preview cannot substitute for those checks on the intended setup.

What to send in an event-game brief

A concise brief with a venue sketch and screen specifications gives design and development a shared starting point. Keep confirmed facts separate from assumptions so outstanding dependencies can be resolved early.

  • The event objective, audience and intended length of a round.
  • The number of participants and how they join.
  • Screen dimensions, orientation, resolution and viewing distance.
  • The controls, devices, connectivity and operator arrangement.
  • The start, play, result and reset behavior.
  • Brand assets, language needs, rehearsal dates and delivery responsibilities.
